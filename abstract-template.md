# Poster abstract template — SURFin students

A poster abstract is **~250 words in six moves**. Each move is a fixed number of
sentences with a fixed job. Write them in order, one at a time, and do not let one
move do another one's work.

The word budget below adds to 250. Treat it as a target, not a rule — but if a
section is running at double its budget, something belongs in a different section.

| # | Section | Sentences | Words | The one question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Title | 1 | ~12 | What did you find? |
| 1 | Background | 1–2 | ~40 | Why would anyone care? |
| 2 | Question | 1 | ~20 | What exactly did you ask? |
| 3 | Methods | 2–3 | ~50 | What did you do, to whom, how many times? |
| 4 | Results | 4–6 | ~100 | What are the numbers? |
| 5 | Conclusion | 1–2 | ~30 | What does it mean? |
| 6 | Caveat / next step | 1 | ~15 | What would you do next? |

---

## 0. Title

State the **finding**, not the topic. A title with a verb in it is usually a
finding; a title that is a noun phrase is usually a topic.

- Topic (weak): *"Effects of FEF inactivation on value-based saccades"*
- Finding (strong): *"Reward value modulates saccade execution, not initiation,
  during FEF inactivation"*

If your result is a null, say so in the title rather than hiding it.

---

## 1. Background — 1 to 2 sentences

Sentence 1: the one fact about the system that a non-specialist needs.
Sentence 2: the **gap** — what is not known, phrased so your question answers it.

Do not review the literature. You have two sentences; spend them on the single
thread that leads to your question.

> *The frontal eye field (FEF) is required for voluntary saccades, but it is
> unclear whether its contribution is uniform across targets or depends on what
> the target is worth.*

**Traps:** starting with "Attention has long fascinated neuroscientists."
Citations. Any sentence that would be true whether or not you did this project.

---

## 2. Question — 1 sentence

One sentence, beginning *"We asked whether…"* or *"We tested whether…"*.

It must be **falsifiable** and must name the comparison you actually ran. If a
reader cannot tell from this sentence what a "no" would have looked like, rewrite it.

> *We asked whether silencing the FEF damages saccades to a high-value object as
> much as saccades to a low-value one.*

**Trap:** a question broader than your data ("we investigated the role of the FEF
in decision-making"). Ask the question your design can answer.

---

## 3. Methods — 2 to 3 sentences

In this order, and nothing else:

1. **Subject and preparation** — species, N animals/subjects, what was recorded.
2. **Task** — what the subject did on one trial, with the numbers that matter
   (eccentricity, delay, how many conditions).
3. **Manipulation and design** — what you changed, how conditions were assigned,
   and **how many trials/cells/sessions** the analysis rests on.

Anyone reading should be able to tell whether your comparison is *within* subject
and whether it has a **control condition**. Name the control explicitly — the
untreated side, the pre-injection block, the sham, whatever it is.

> *In one session (1740 correct trials), a monkey made saccades to fractal objects
> signalling large or small reward, presented pseudo-randomly at 20° in either
> hemifield, while muscimol was injected unilaterally into the FEF and a single FEF
> neuron was recorded throughout.*

**Traps:** listing software. Describing analyses you will name again in Results.
Omitting N.

---

## 4. Results — 4 to 6 sentences

The core. **Every claim carries a number.** The pattern for one result sentence is:

> [condition] [direction of change] [effect size with units] ([the control, with
> its number]; [statistic, p]).

Order the sentences as a story, strongest first:

1. **Manipulation check** — show the manipulation worked at all (the neuron was
   suppressed, the drug took effect, the training reached criterion). One sentence.
2. **Main effect** — the biggest, clearest thing that changed, *with the control
   comparison inside the same sentence*.
3. **The dissociation or interaction** — where the interesting structure is: two
   measures that behave differently, or an effect present in one condition and
   absent in another.
4. **The key negative** — what did *not* change. This is what makes the positive
   result specific rather than general fatigue/drift/noise.
5. **The test that settles it** — the statistic you would defend, named with its
   correction: *"Holm-corrected p < 0.001, confirmed by a permutation test on
   medians."*

> *The neuron's visual response fell from 22.6 to 13.3 spikes/s during the
> injection and partially recovered (18.0). Contralateral reaction time slowed by
> 35 ms (ipsilateral +3.5 ms; interaction p < 0.001)… Only saccade duration showed
> a value-gap change confined to the affected side (−12.2 ms, Holm-corrected
> p < 0.001).*

**Traps:**
- "Results will be discussed." Never. Write the abstract after you have the numbers.
- A p-value with no effect size. `p < 0.001` says the effect is detectable, not
  that it is big. Give both.
- A change with no control. "RT increased by 35 ms" is uninterpretable until the
  reader knows what the untreated side did.
- Reporting a difference that was **already there at baseline** as if you caused it.

---

## 5. Conclusion — 1 to 2 sentences

Restate the finding one level up in abstraction, and **no further**. The conclusion
must follow from the sentences directly above it.

Useful test: cross out your Results section. If the Conclusion still sounds
supported, it is too vague; if it sounds unsupported, it is too strong. You want it
to sound *exactly as strong as the numbers*.

> *FEF inactivation therefore interacts with reward value through how movements are
> executed rather than how quickly they are initiated.*

**Trap:** "These results shed light on the neural basis of decision-making." That
sentence fits any abstract, which means it belongs to none of them.

---

## 6. Caveat or next step — 1 sentence (optional but recommended)

One honest limit and the experiment that would remove it. This makes you look like
a scientist rather than a student, and it is the sentence judges ask about.

> *The effect rests on one measure in a single session; a second injection later in
> the session would separate the drug's action from time on task.*

---

## Before you submit — checklist

- [ ] Every numerical claim has **units** and an **effect size**, not only a p-value.
- [ ] Every effect has its **control** stated in the same sentence.
- [ ] N appears somewhere (trials, cells, sessions, subjects).
- [ ] No sentence promises results instead of giving them.
- [ ] The Conclusion uses no word ("encodes", "controls", "causes") that the design
      cannot support.
- [ ] A first-year student could read the Background; a specialist could check the
      Results.
- [ ] Read it aloud once. Anything you stumble over, rewrite.
- [ ] Word count is within ~10% of the limit the meeting gave you.

## Working method

1. Write **Results** first, as bare bullet points with the numbers copied from your
   analysis. No prose.
2. Write the **Conclusion** that those bullets force on you.
3. Write the **Question** that the Conclusion answers.
4. Write the **Background** that makes the Question worth asking.
5. Write **Methods** last — by then you know exactly which details the Results need.
6. Turn the bullets into sentences, then cut 20% of the words.
